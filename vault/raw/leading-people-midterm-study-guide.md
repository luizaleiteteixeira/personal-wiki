Midterm Study Guide — Leading People (Complete)

# Introduction: Leadership vs. Management (Session 1)

  - Management = organize/coordinate/execute → about process/how
  - Leadership = influence/vision/why → about direction/meaning
  - Great leaders do both
  - Becoming a good leader might require being a good manager
  - Example: Sully (Hudson landing) = competence + calm vision

# Managing People (Session 2)

  - AI & Management: Coordination, data-processing and monitoring tasks get automated first; managers’ value shifts to judgment (interpreting), ethics (moral decisions), coaching, meaning-making to human leadership (motivating people).
  - Google’s Project Oxygen (8 behaviors of a great manager): Good coach; empowers (no micromanage); cares about people; results-oriented; good communicator; supports careers; clear vision/strategy; technical expertise.
  - Big-Two
  - Agency: competence, dominance
  - Communality: warmth, trustworthiness → less likely to self-select into leadership word
  - Evidence-Based Management (EBM): Instead of anecdotes and bias, use systematic evidence - people mistake correlation for causation.
  - Good evidence: experiments → random assignment, control groups.
  - Basics of Experimental Design: Hallmarks of a good experiment: Independent variable (what the researcher changes), dependent variable (what’s being measured to test the hypothesis), random sample and presence of a control group.
  - Common pitfalls in organizational experiments:
  - Contamination: people in the control group hear about the treatment.
  - Small sample size: chance results.
  - Hawethorne effect: behavior changes because people know they’re being studied.
  - Attrition: participants drop out unevenly.

# Knowing Yourself & Others (Session 3)

  - Authenticity = when behavior reflects the inner self
  - Experienced authenticity (you feel aligned) → higher engagement, lower intent to exit AND Perceived authenticity (others see you as genuine) → trust & effectiveness. Tension: “be yourself” vs. role expectations - not often related
  - Authentic leader based on self-awareness and values-based leadership
  - People are not always themselves: Impression Management Strategy: Self-promotion, Ingratiation, Supplication, Exemplification.
  - Personality is part of how you see yourself but also how others make sense of who you are
  - Personality test: Big Five is the most valid (OCEAN): Openness (creativity), Conscientiousness (performance predictor), Extraversion (leadership/sales), Agreeableness (collaboration), Neuroticism (emotionally reactive).

# Making Wise Decisions (Session 4)

  - Multi-attribute utility technique - always define attributes before having the options. Problem - criteria (weight) - alternatives - rate each one - optimal decision
  - Why don’t we make rational decisions? → Bounded Rationality: People satisfice due to limits of time/attention.
  - Heuristics: shortcuts in how human store, process and retrieve information, but it can generate bias
  - Representativeness: Ignore base rates → We judge the likelihood of something based on how much it resembles our stereotype/prototype — instead of considering actual statistics (base rates)
  - Common errors:
  - Base Rate Neglect: ignoring actual frequency (e.g., decease probability)
  - Conjunction Fallacy: assuming two events together are more likely than one alone (e.g., Linda problem).
  - Misconceptions of Chance: expecting short sequences to “look random.”
  - Regression to the Mean: failing to recognize extreme performance naturally moves back toward average.
  - Workplace example: Assuming a charismatic candidate must be a good leader because they “look like” other successful leaders, ignoring base rates of performance.
  - How to challenge: using data
  - Availability: vivid/easy-to-recall events feel more likely → We judge frequency or probability based on how easily we can recall, process or imagine examples.
  - Common errors:
  - Ease of recall: vivid/graphic events overweighted (plane crash vs. car crash).
  - Retrievability: things more salient in memory feel more common.
  - Presumed associations: assuming two things co-occur just because they’re linked in memory (e.g., stereotypes).
  - Workplace example: A manager fears cybersecurity breaches more than supply chain issues because news about hacks is vivid, even though supply chain risks are statistically more likely.
  - How to challenge: compare against benchmark, rely on documentation instead of memory
  - Anchoring & Adjustment: first number sticks; adjustments too small → Our estimates and judgments are overly influenced by the first number, impression, or idea we see — and we adjust insufficiently from it. We stop adjusting once our estimate feels “plausible.”
  - Example: price of German car - people guessed differently depending on the initial number given
  - Workplace example: A company sets its target based on a competitor benchmarking even if it’s not plausible for them
  - How to challenge: write number before
  - Framing / Prospect Theory: choices shift with wording (gains vs. losses).
  - Gain frame: Risk-averse (prefer sure small gain).
  - Loss frame: Risk-seeking (prefer gamble to avoid certain loss).
  - Example: Gain frame: “Save 200 jobs” → people choose certainty. VS Loss frame: “Lose 400 jobs” → people choose gamble.
  - Workplace example: Presenting a restructuring as “preserving 70% of positions” vs. “cutting 30%.”
  - How to challenge: consider same options from different perspectives
  - Egocentrism: Overestimating the extent to which others see, understand, and agree with our perspective.
  - Example: Thinking your contribution to a group project is larger than others think it is.
  - How to challenge: practice seeing other’s perspective, where they are coming from
  - Fundamental Attribution Error (FAE): Mistake in cause attribution → Over-attributing others’ behavior to personality/disposition and underweighting situational
  - Examples: “Sam was late → he’s irresponsible” (ignores that BART was delayed).
  - Confirmation Bias: We look for, interpret, and remember evidence that confirms what we already believe — and ignore or discount contrary evidence.
  - Example: A manager who believes remote workers are less productive only notices late email replies, ignoring evidence of high-quality output.
  - How to challenge: look for disconfirming information - prove me wrong
  - Sampling on the Dependent Variable: only successes/failures considered → Looking only at successful (or failed) cases and drawing conclusions, without comparing to the opposite cases. Not considering all the data.
  - Examples: Studying only successful companies to find “what works” — but maybe failed companies did the same thing; F1 story: only checking when problems happened, but not success; Copying practices of “top salespeople” without checking if less successful reps did the same.
  - How to challenge: check four cells: failures, success, and both alternatives

# Motivation & Incentives (Session 5)

  - Expectancy Theory: Motivation = Expectancy (can I do it? Effort + Performance) × Instrumentality (will performance be rewarded? Performance + Reward) × Valence (do I value the reward?). Diagnose which link is broken.
  - Keys to motivation
  - Goals: Specific + challenging + self-set goals boost motivation - SMART. Try to break into small habits, and always enforce it. Revit big picture
  - Risks:
  - Hedonic Treadmill (ever-rising goals - thinking you will be perfectly happy when achieving something)
  - Overly aggressive goals can backfire
  - Fresh Start Effect at landmarks (e.g., New Year).
  - Feedback: Candid, specific, future-focused and problem-solving focused. Positive feedback enhances commitment and helps novices; constructive challenge helps committed experts to reflect.
  - Incentives: rewards & Meaning
  - Extrinsic rewards matter early ($, bonus, praise) - operational task, people still not committed to task
  - Intrinsic rewards sustain performance
  - Passion, purpose, and meaning. Examples: impact, flexibility, role expansion

# Groups & Teams (Session 6)

  - Problem solving = ask questions!
  - Why Teams Win (when set up right): Aggregate information, divide and conquer, leverage expertise - if diversity, independence, and decentralization hold.
  - How Groups Fail: Homogeneity, deference to power, quick consensus → expertise suppressed; wisdom of crowds collapses.
  - Social Influence: Conformity (match peers) and compliance (yield to pressure). Helpful for speed; harmful for truth-seeking.
  - Groupthink: Strong cohesion + pressure for unanimity → members fail to think critically and reject influence of others.
  - Fix with dissent, devil’s advocate, anonymous input and creating a psychological safety space
  - Psychological Safety: Shared belief it’s safe to speak up, admit mistakes and take risks. Safer teams may report more errors, but learn faster, perform better. Build it by flattening hierarchy, norms, equal participation, trust…

Hiring & Firing (Session 7)

Hiring

  - Grogan Air case: Success required sharing unique info
  - Common Information Effect: What everyone knows gets discussed more, but unique info drives better decisions.
  - Structural fixes: Decide info & criteria in advance, break process into stages, avoid early votes, give equal time, assign a dissenter.
  - Common traps:
  - Hiring reactively (replace old person instead of role needs).
  - Hiring unrealistically (overqualified).
  - Hiring people similar to yourself
  - Referrals - referrers do the onboarding and might create a less diverse environment
  - Best practices:
  - Retention
  - Tracking performance and success rate
  - Process: Post internally, structured interviews, multiple predictors, systematic scoring, consider probationary periods.
  - AI hiring: Candidates adjust self-presentation if they know AI is screening (more “analytical,” less “human”).

Firing

  - Before the decision: Hire well, give feedback early, make firing the last step.
  - Conversation: Deliver decision in 30 sec, clear and direct, professional but empathetic, don’t over-defend, prepare for emotion.
  - After: Don’t shift blame, be generous with severance, explain the decision to the team within confidentiality limits.
  - Fairness & Justice

  - People accept negative outcomes if process is perceived as fair
  - Distributive justice: outcomes are made fairly (equity, need)
  - Procedural justice: procedure/process fair, unbiased, consistent, accurate, transparent.
