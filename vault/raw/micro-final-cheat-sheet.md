|Ƹ| < 1 → inelastic (not very) → large change in P - Q almost same (necessities or unique products) - when P < P*
|Ƹ| = 0 → perfect inelastic (I) / |Ƹ| = 1 → unit elastic (E)

MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION
Differentiated products (but closely related/substituted); large numbers of sellers and buyers, price makers (subs prices and availability can affect demand), no barriers. Ex: restaurants, hotels
ST: price as a monopolist (quantity when MR=MC)
LR: demand shifts down with entry of similar products that split customers - more options appear (more elastic), until MR=MC and P=AC - no profit
Price making power is not enough to ensure profitability. What sustains is restricted/costly entry + advantage that cannot be replicated (lower costs - natural advantage, government subsidy, bending rules.
A = Profit
B = costs
A+B = revenue
1 - companies leaving
2 - companies entering the market
Equilibrium → MC = AC = P*

PERFECT COMPETITION (Price-takers)
Identical products (commodities, homogeneous), large number of sellers/buyers, price takers (consumers don’t chance with their actions), no barriers to entry. Ex: agricultural
ST: supply = demand → P + MC. Remember to consider aggregate (x number of firms)
LR: no economic profit (no firm wants to enter or leave); supply horizontal line
P* = minimum AC = AC = MC
Firms make just enough money to cover all their costs, including opportunity cost of capital
MR > MC → increase Q; MR < MR → decrease Q

PRICE DISCRIMINATION
Charge different prices not due to cost differences
Goal: capture consumer surplus and eliminate deadweight loss
First-degree (perfect): Each consumer charged WTP. Rare in practice, requires perfect information
Third-degree (segment/group): Groups face different prices (ex: students, seniors, geography, business).
Conditions: 1) Different buyers have different WTP; 2) Ability to identify who has high/low WTP; 3) Ability to segment buyers with different WTP.
Restrictions: Arbitrage, legal
Second-degree (menu): offering different versions of a product to introduce lower/higher value buyers to reveal themselves
Downside: Not perfect (CS and DWL - has to give value to consumers)
Advantage: no arbitrage and no need to identify/segment market
Versioning (ex: Freemium - Spotify) - quality
(1) Choose the low price for low quality; (2) Check the higher willingness to pay for high quality and give the same surplus as if they choose the LQ option; (3) Check if profit is higher than selling only to higher WTP
(WPhq - Phq) >= (WPlq - Plq)
Condition: difference in WTP for upgrade is higher for high type
Ex: hobbling - damage/downgrade products to make it less appealing to high types
Bundling (pure - only bundle or mixed - also individual) - composition
Solve for all individual prices and bundle
Goal: Decrease deadweight loss
Usually more profitable when MC is lower (digital content)
Condition: WTP for bundle is more similar than for individual prices; consumers are heterogeneous (different types of buyers have a high WTP for different products (neither is high type for both goods)); negative correlation in WTP across goods and types
Quantity discount (menu pricing, but for different quantity)
(1) price for LQ equal to low WTP, (2) price for HQ that makes high type indifferent, (3) Check if price is higher if selling only high or low quantity
When more units, P has to incorporate CS to the first unit
Condition: seller wants to sell larger Q to the high type

GAME THEORY
Row = left number / column = right numbers
Strategy = best response given the belief of other player’s choice
Dominant: always best option no matter other’s choice
Dominated: other option is at least as good
Nash equilibrium (NE): no player wants to change their strategy. C: every player choose their best strategy, beliefs are correct
Prisoner's Dilemma: players have dominant strategy to do one thing, but there is an alternative that would be better for both. Inescapable bad outcome, as the dominant is not the one that maximizes surplus

AUCTIONS
Win: CS = WTP - P. Lose: CS = 0
Second-price (sealed): winner pays the bid of second-highest
Clear vision of the value: Bidding your value is the dominant strategy, CS otherwise will always be lower
If bid lower, someone can bid your value and you would have more surplus bidding more (leaving money on the table)
If bid more and the second person bids more than your value, CS will be negative
~English auction (ascending) (if people were rational)
Advantage: give bidders a really clear strategic space (as they have a dominant strategy) - assuming they know their value
First-price (sealed): winner pays their bid
Best strategy is to bid below your value to keep surplus (CS>0)
Shade to trade-off win-probability and profit if winning
~ Dutch auction (descending)
Other details: people can value things differently (common vs private values); incomplete vs complete information. Sealed bid - you don’t know what other players are playing
Winner’s curse: when information is imperfect (unsure of the value), observation is that winner tends to overpay because they have the belief that the value of the object is higher than the truth (often inaccurate, otherwise others would agree). Whoever has the bias upward wins the auction, but pays too much. To avoid: Shade your value

OLIGOPOLY
Few competitors (small number of price-makers), barriers to entry, long-run potential for positive economic profits.
Conditions: large fixed cost, resource limitation, policy/restriction (regulation, limited subsidies). Ex: aircrafts, appliances
Bertrand competition: firms compete in price
Elements: identical product, limited number of sellers but many buyers, price maker, no entry/exit (# of firms is fixed)
Optimal would be MR=MC, but if a firm lowers the price, gets all the Q, so price goes down until P=MC (nash equilibrium) - no profit
Bertrand Trap = Prisoner's dilemma. At any P>MC, dominant strategy to undercut even if it leads to zero profit (an outcome that both firms are worse off). Conditions and how to escape to sustain profitability:
Avoid BT by avoiding conditions - change the game!
No cost advantage → find a cost advantage. Innovate in production, partnership with suppliers, using competitors data to launch new product without R&D cost, negotiation and taxes
No differentiation (identical products) → make your product different. Differentiation leads to profitability: Firm can MR=MC - taking in consideration the competitor's price - add in the demand/revenue function). Knowing that their price affect your demand curve, you can then find the best response choice of P
Graph with Pa in X and Pb in Y. NE is in the intersection (best response at the same time)
No customer lock-in (no switching costs) → lock in customers and make them loyal. Make it difficult to go away after choosing a product. Ex: loyalty programs, termination fee, brand identity, taste/style/comfort
Risk: spend so much money to lock customer, that if need to compete for lower prices/discounts, can risk the profitability of business
Prices are known to all customers → make it hard to compare prices. Reduce price transparency, making it difficult to see/understand (ex: consulting services)
Unlimited capacity (you can sell whatever is demanded) → commit to capacity. Cournot model: firms compete in Q
Q is a limitation (ex: hotel rooms), each company faces a residual demand function (demand that remains after competitor sells) - demand shifts down
Firms best response / NE is when Qa = Qb (MR=MC and they have symmetrical functions)
LR: you can end up with positive profit
You can't commit not to undercut → collude. Cooperation / collusion is usually illegal, but firms can see what price others are charging and charge the same - forms of "tacit collusion" (actions)
Your strategy can depend on what happened in prior rounds - "tit-for-tat" (doing what others do, focusing on maximizing payoff in general)
Edgeworth cycles: price leadership and others following
Price matching avoid undercut and sustain price collusion, as companies will split the market anyway
Avoid competition → Deter or eliminate competition (acquisition, merger, price really low so competitors go off business and then raise price again, lobbying - regulation and barriers to entry). The treat has to be credible to be effective
Cournot produce less than Bertrand, but more than Monopoly
Quantity: Bertrand > Cournot > Monopoly
More production → Less production
Profit: Bertrand < Cournot < Monopoly
