## **1.1 What Is Marketing?**

**Marketing** \= creating, communicating, delivering value to a chosen target. People buy expectations, not things  
**Not** product-first. **Marketing is customer-first**.

### **Marketing vs. Product POV**

* *Product POV:* “We make X, so we need to sell it.”  
* *Marketing POV:* “Customers need X, so we will make and communicate it.”

**EXAMPLES**  
*Discussed in class:* Steve Jobs — consumers don’t know what they want until you show them.  
*Real:* Airbnb shifted from “book rooms” → “Belong anywhere.”  
*Made-up:* A water bottle company decides that its purpose is “supporting daily hydration rituals,” not “selling bottles.”  

---

## **1.2 Brand Strategy Essentials**

**Brand \= promise \+ expectation \+ experience.**  
 Brands exist in the consumer’s mind.

### **Frameworks**

* **5C’s** (Company, Customer, Competitor, Collaborators, Context)  
* **Funnel:** Awareness → Interest → Consideration → Intent → Evaluation → Purchase

**Brand:**  stands independent of product 

* **Brand Equity:** mental associations \+ perceived value, loyal users  
* **Brand Imagery:** emotional layer of positioning  
* **Brand Architecture:**  
  * House of Brands (P\&G)  
  * Branded House (Virgin)  
  * Hybrid (Coca-Cola, Kraft)

**EXAMPLES**

* *Discussed:* Clif vs. PowerBar imagery.  
* *Real:* Patagonia stands for activism → high loyalty.  
* *Made-up:* A fintech brand positions itself around “planning freedom,” not budgeting.

# **2\. SEGMENTATION, TARGETING, POSITIONING (STP)**

---

# **2.1 Segmentation**

**Segmentation \=** process of dividing the universe into actionable, meaningful groups.  
	Process of identifying & analyzing potential.  Vs target as the end result.

### **Best practice: Start with**

1. **Occasions**  
2. **Benefits sought:** Value proposition  
3. Then refine with:  
   1. demographics  
   2. psychographics  
   3. behaviors  
   4. loyalty  
   5. Attitudes  
4. Select more attractive segment(s)

### **Criteria for effective segmentation**

1. Homogeneous within segments  
2. Heterogeneous between segments  
3. Substantial  
4. Reachable

### **Segmentation Studies Examples**

* **Discussed:** Levi’s segments (Denim diehards, Classic loyalists, Fashion leaders, Fashion novices).  
* **Real:** Starbucks segments by beverage ritual (morning commuter; afternoon treat).  
* **Made-up:** Meal-kit service segments by “value-driven cooks,” “time-starved professionals,” “nutrition optimizers.”

---

## **2.2 Targeting**

**Targeting \= choosing who to go after.**  
Why target?

* More **efficient** (condense spend)  
* More **effective** (tailored messages)

### **Targeting Variables**

* Demographics  
* Psychographics  
* Behavioral  
* Geography  
* Broader buyer behavior (decision systems, information processing)

### **BDI/CDI:** 

* How developed is the brand and category in the area.   
  * CDI \= category development index.    
    * % category sales in region/%US population in region  
  * BDI \= brand development index.    
    * % US brand sales in region/ % US population in region  
  * High BDI/low CDI \- maintenance/ defensive  
  * Low BDI/High CDI \- best growth opportunity

### **CLV**

* Customer lifetime value is true measure of value, repeat loyalty  
* CLV \= (M x EL) \- CAC  
  * Margin (M) \= revenue \- direct cost  
  * Expected lifetime (EL) \= 1/churn rate  
  * Customer acquisition cost  
    

### **Other Target issues**

* **Influencers**: Purchaser, decision maker and influencers may be different  
* **B2B**: multiple influencers and decision makers. Have to identify appropriate contact  
* **Source of volume**: where are new users coming from

### **Examples**

* *Discussed:* Best Buy “devil customers” NOT targeted.  
* *Real:* Nike targets “serious runners” in specialty stores.  
* *Made-up:* A pet food startup targets “first-time puppy owners wanting guidance.”

---

## **2.3 Positioning**

**Positioning \= place a brand occupies in the consumer’s mind.**  
 Simple, relevant, value-based.

### **Great Positioning Has:**

* Clear **benefit**  
* Clear **target**  
* Clear **frame of reference**  
* **Differentiation**  
* **Reason to believe**

### **Examples of Positioning Statements**

#### **1\. *Discussed in class:***

* **Clif Bar** \= natural energy for outdoor athletes.  
* **PowerBar** \= performance fuel for serious competitors.

#### **2\. *Real companies:***

* **Apple:** creativity \+ simplicity \+ premium experience.  
* **IKEA:** functional design at accessible prices.

#### **3\. *Made-up:***

* **EcoClean detergent:** “For eco-conscious families who want a safe clean—EcoClean is the natural detergent that delivers strong performance without toxic ingredients.”

***4\. Cheat sheet:***

* For \[target\], \[brand\] is the \[frame of reference\] that \[primary benefit\], because \[reason to believe\].  
* 21Seeds: For women who want light, social cocktails, 21Seeds is the infused tequila brand that makes tequila approachable and fun, because it is smooth, flavorful, and designed for easy mixing.  
* Nike: For serious runners, Nike is the performance brand that helps athletes push their limits, because it combines innovation, data, and elite athlete insight.  
* Shake Shack: For urban consumers who value quality and experience, Shake Shack is the modern burger brand that elevates fast food, because it combines premium ingredients with a strong sense of place.  
* Zoom: For teams and individuals who need frictionless communication, Zoom is the video-conferencing platform that just works, because it prioritizes simplicity and reliability.  
* Teams: For organizations already using Microsoft tools, Teams is the collaboration platform that centralizes communication and work, because it is deeply integrated into the Office ecosystem.

# **3.1 Differentiation**

Differentiation can come from any of the 4Ps — not just product.

### **Ways to Differentiate**

* Product features  
* Imagery  
* Packaging  
* UX  
* Distribution (In-N-Out, Zara)

**Examples**

* *Discussed:* iPad → redefined category.  
* *Real:* Oura ring differentiates with design \+ wellness insight.  
* *Made-up:* A travel app differentiates by offering “emotion-based itineraries.”

---

# **3.2 Levels of Product (Core → Expected → Augmented)**

* **Core:** the need (communication, hydration, mobility), the basic product  
* **Expected:** physical product  
* **Augmented:** added services, loyalty programs, warranties.  Augmented has become the expectation (competition \> comparison \> conformity)  
  * By addition \= features  
  * By multiplication \= product portfolio  
  * By reversal \= take something away (Ikea)  
* **Potential:** next step of the product

**Examples**

* *Discussed:* Phones, Spotify augmentation.  
    
* *Real:* Amazon Prime (augmented).  
* *Made-up:* Minimalist airline removes screens to emphasize low fares.

---

# **3.3 Product Life Cycle (PLC)**

Stages: Intro → Growth → Maturity → Decline  
Use PLC for **strategic planning**.

### **Key insights**

* Intro \= high promotion spending  
  * Trying to find info, trial  
  * Building distribution channels, target early adopters (risk-takers and less price sensitive)  
* Growth \= competitors enter  
  * Promotional spending still high, but starting to profit (prices high, cost decreasing because of scale)  
  * Fight for MS  
* Maturity \= levels off and focus on competition (new ways to gain shares)  
  * Existing costumes to buy more often  
* Decline \= prune, reposition, or niche strategy. Hard to reverse.   
  * Can still find volume \- most competitors leave, a few continue  
      
* Elasticity changes. Start inelastic, become more sensitive with more competitors, and then less elastic again

**Examples**

* *Discussed:* Spotify (growth → maturity tension).  
* *Real:* Converse revival  
* *Made-up:* Wearable baby trackers entering maturity with heavy competitor entry.

---

# **3.4 Category Management**

* When declining, can disinvest in some and focus on higher revenue  
* Breadth vs. depth  
* Line extension vs. brand extension  
* Cannibalization evaluation  
* Brand architecture links

**Examples**

* *Real:* Coca-Cola’s portfolio choices.  
* *Made-up:* A yogurt brand adds plant-based SKUs to extend depth.

# **4\. PLACE (DISTRIBUTION / CHANNELS)**

---

## **4.1 Intermediaries & Channel Structure**

Intermediaries (link producers with buyers) add value via: Access, Storage, Logistics, Aggregation  
Examples: big retailers, brokers \+ smaller stores, two sided platforms (Airbnb, Doordash)

**Conventional vs. VMS**

* Conventional \= retailers. Each partner independent   
* Vertical Marketing systems (VMS) \= aligned via ownership/contract (Zara, Kroger)

---

## **4.2 Disintermediation & Reintermediation**

Cutting intermediaries (DTC) or adding new ones (platforms) \- gives more control over process  
Only makes sense to have intermediaries if they add value

* Low end \- online channel, easier, lower cost VS high end \- dedicated people, money, time, higher dollar value

**Examples**

* *Discussed:* Travel industry (online platforms and less travel agencies), Costco (purchased a meat company and now does meat processing), Netflix (distributed for studios, but they are also competitors), Gap (was Levi’s only chain, but now a big player).  
* *Real:* Warby Parker; Shopify enabling new intermediaries.  
* *Made-up:* DTC supplements brand drops Amazon to go exclusive with a wellness retailer.

---

## **4.3 Omnichannel**

Consumers switch channels: Online-first, physical-first, switchers.  
Segment based on **shopping attitudes**, not demographics.  
---

## **4.4 Channel Conflict**

Horizontal conflict \=  same level w/in system 

* Ex. CPG in supermarket (Safeway vs CVS)

Vertical conflict \= within channel

* Ex. American express resistance from merchant fees  
* Franchises, dealership

**Power dynamics (Costco, Walmart).**  
---

## **4.5 Push vs Pull**

Push \= intermediaries push product  
Pull \= consumers demand brand  
Do we create a brand that is sufficient to attract customers or do we need to rely on intermediaries?

### **Examples**

---

## **5.3 Pricing Strategies**

* Penetration \- low initial price to penetrate market quickly  
  * Difficult to sustain as competitors can match  
* Skimming \- high initial price to skim revenue   
  * Leader/follower  
  * Attention to timing \- competitors might low price and lose share

* Psychological pricing

---

## **5.4 Pricing Models**

* Freemium (Dropbox, LinkedIn)

* Captive products  
* Surge pricing (lower price at peak hours to attract new customers / higher price in peak hours)

  HOW TO PRICE?  
* Strategy (communication to consumers)  
* Cost plus (cost \+ desired margin)  
  * Real state: house is price \+ restoration  
* Consumer perception and WTP (Market driven) \- what do they value and want?  
  * Luxury companies (perceived)  
* Breakeven (no profit)

# **6\. PROMOTION & COMMUNICATIONS**

---

## **6.1 Promotion Objectives**

Must be **measurable** and tied to specific behavior change (controllable).  
Inform \> persuade \> remind  
Awareness \> Interest \> Desire \> Action  
---

## **6.2 Communication Models**

* **AIDA** (Awareness → Interest → Desire → Action)  
* Narrative \+ emotion \> rational alone

---

## **6.3 Messaging**

* **Single-minded message** (simple) \- straight-forward, easy to remember  
* Can use multiple medias to different messages  
  Consumer POV.  
  Not explaining → motivating and making consumers take action.  
* Continuum \- has to have a rational, but emotional is also important  
* Rational vs emotional messaging: emotional drives people better  
* Media Strategy \- reach vs frequency. Has to manage and control flow of information  
* Create a narrative

---

## **6.4 Brand vs. Promotion**

* Brand \= long-term; builds equity. Focus on loyalty  
* Promotion \= short-term; drives action  
* Tension: promotions can erode value over time

---

## **6.5 Creative Brief**

* A creative brief is a short strategic document that translates marketing strategy into a clear communication direction. It ensures that all messaging is focused on one core idea that should stay in the consumer’s mind.  
* A strong creative brief defines:  
  * Target: who we are trying to influence  
  * Insight / Problem: the key human tension or truth  
  * Communication Objective: what the message should accomplish  
  * Frame of Reference: the category context  
  * SMIP (Single Most Important Point): the one message to remember  
  * RTB (Reason to Believe): why the message is credible  
  * Tone: how the brand should sound and feel  
  * The creative brief is not the ad itself, a slogan, or a media plan. It is the strategic foundation that guides all creative execution.  
* If a campaign feels confusing or unfocused, the problem is usually a weak or unclear creative brief.

---

## **6.6 PR & Packaging**

* PR \= low cost, high credibility, low control  
* Design \= logo design can be instrumental to brand/ corporate identity   
* Packaging \= crucial for shelf brands

## **7.4 Buyer Behavior Concepts**

* System 1  
  * Fast, intuitive, emotional  
  * Automatic decisions  
  * Low effort  
  * Dominates most daily purchases  
  * Examples: Choosing Zoom because it “just works”, Picking a familiar brand on the shelf  
* System 2  
  * Slow, deliberate, rational  
  * High effort  
  * Used for complex or high-risk decisions  
  * Examples: Choosing an MBA program, Buying a car  
* What people say ≠ what they do.  
* Biases  
* Culture effects (communication, individualistic vs collectivist culture, hierarchy…)  
  Motivation  
  * Intrinsic \- Driven by enjoyment, meaning, identity. More sustainable  
  * Extrinsic \- Driven by rewards, discounts, pressure. Short term

# **13\. CASE SUMMARIES**

# **13.1 Pulse Fitness — “Who Is the Strongest Segment?”**

### **Summary**

Pulse Fitness evaluates which customer segment—Bodybuilders, Social Exercisers, or Young Professionals—offers the best long-term value. Although the segments differ in size and behavior, the strongest segment is the one with the **highest CLV**, not the most obvious demographic.

### **Concepts Illustrated**

* Benefits- and behavior-based segmentation  
* CLV and customer profitability  
* Targeting based on economics, not intuition  
* Core idea from *Marketing Imagination*: people buy **solutions**, not memberships

### **Key Takeaways**

* Larger segments do *not* equal better targets.  
* Segmentation must be connected to **economic outcomes** (retention, visit frequency, CAC).  
* Correct targeting improves acquisition and retention efficiency.

### **Strategic Insights**

Pulse Fitness shows that **the most strategic segment is the one you can serve profitably**, not the one with the biggest population or the one you personally identify with.  
---

# **13.2 Vans — “Should We Lean Into Sustainability?”**

### **Summary**

Vans considers intensifying its sustainability initiatives to appeal to Gen Z. Although sustainability is a rising cultural value, its fit with Vans’ brand—rooted in creativity, rebellion, and action sports—is unclear.

### **Concepts Illustrated**

* Brand positioning & brand fit  
* Cause marketing  
* Attitude–behavior gap  
* Augmentation vs repositioning  
* Emotional brand equity

### **Key Takeaways**

* Cause initiatives only work when they **reinforce brand meaning**.  
* Sustainability may attract customers but risks diluting Vans’ identity.  
* Consumers claim they value sustainability, but **actual behavior is inconsistent**.

### **Strategic Insights**

Sustainability can serve as an **augmentation layer**, but should *not* replace Vans’ core identity. The brand must integrate sustainability in a way that feels consistent with skate culture and self-expression.  
---

# **13.3 21Seeds — “Taking Shots at Growth”**

### **Summary**

21Seeds, a female-founded flavored tequila brand, grows rapidly by appealing to women seeking lighter, approachable cocktail options. As it scales, the company must protect its authenticity while expanding distribution.

### **Concepts Illustrated**

* Go-To-Market strategy (GTM)  
* White-space targeting & occasion-based segmentation  
* Emotional/lifestyle positioning  
* Storytelling as differentiation  
* Augmentation (smooth, cocktail-ready tequila)

### **Key Takeaways**

* GTM success depends on **target clarity**, **messaging**, and **high-control initial channels**.  
* Growth requires balancing **scale** and **authenticity**.  
* Lifestyle positioning differentiates in a mature, crowded category.

### **Strategic Insights**

21Seeds shows that insight-driven segmentation (“women who want fun, approachable tequila”) creates new category entry points. Success depends more on **story \+ brand world** than product specs.  
---

# **13.4 Shake Shack — “Should We Move Away From Beef?”**

### **Summary**

Shake Shack debates offering more chicken and plant-based items amid changing protein preferences. The tension arises between staying true to the “elevated burger” identity and responding to macro health/environment trends.

### **Concepts Illustrated**

* Product Life Cycle (category maturity)  
* Brand authenticity vs expansion  
* Innovation risk  
* Portfolio diversification

### **Key Takeaways**

* Innovation should **extend** the brand, not contradict it.  
* Even declining categories (beef) can remain central if identity is strong.  
* Customers come to Shack for the **burger ritual**, not just food.

### **Strategic Insights**

Shake Shack must innovate **around** beef, not replace it. Strategic growth comes from **adjacencies** (chicken, veggie, limited editions) that broaden appeal without breaking the brand soul.  
---

# **13.5 AFS — “Unfurling Growth / Next Product Line”**

### **Summary**

AFS, a premium flag manufacturer, explores how to grow beyond its core flagship business. Options include new materials, accessories, premium finishes, or adjacent patriotic products.

### **Concepts Illustrated**

* Category management & adjacency mapping  
* Augmentation (premium stitching, heritage tags)  
* Cannibalization vs expansion  
* Emotional benefits (identity, pride, commemoration)

### **Key Takeaways**

* Augmentation creates **value perception** in symbolic categories.  
* Extensions must reflect the **core emotional job-to-be-done** (patriotism, identity).  
* Cannibalization isn’t a problem if **total portfolio value** increases.

### **Strategic Insights**

AFS wins by deepening meaning, not adding random products. Growth comes from **offering more ways to express identity** — a perfect application of the “people buy solutions, not things” principle.  
---

# **13.6 Tepper School of Business — Rebranding Case**

### **Summary**

Tepper undergoes a full branding refresh to clarify its identity as a school that blends analytics with leadership. The challenge is aligning messages across stakeholders and distilling the identity into a slogan.

### **Concepts Illustrated**

* SMIP (Single Most Important Point)  
* Messaging architecture  
* Positioning translation → tagline  
* Internal alignment and narrative coherence

### **Key Takeaways**

* Rebranding is about **clarity of message**, not color palettes or logos.  
* A slogan is useful only when it expresses the school’s **strategic essence**.  
* A strong brand must unite diverse stakeholders.

### **Strategic Insights**

The Tepper case shows how organizations must simplify complex identities into **one coherent narrative**. Messaging must be insight-driven and emotionally resonant.  
---

# **13.7 Nike Consumer Direct Offense (CDO)**

### **Summary**

Nike accelerates its shift toward DTC, reducing wholesale partnerships to gain control over data, margins, and brand experience. But execution challenges arise: stock shortages, retailer backlash, and reduced physical presence.

### **Concepts Illustrated**

* Channel design & restructuring  
* Push vs Pull  
* Channel conflict  
* Retailer power  
* Omnichannel expectations

### **Key Takeaways**

* DTC raises margins but increases operational complexity.  
* Wholesale partners provide **reach, discovery, and convenience**.  
* A channel shift must match operational readiness.

### **Strategic Insights**

Nike’s case illustrates that channel strategy is a **value chain decision**, not a marketing-only decision. Removing intermediaries can improve efficiency but weaken ecosystem stability.  
---

# **13.8 Microsoft Teams vs Zoom**

### **Summary**

Zoom wins early via simplicity and frictionless onboarding; Teams wins long-term through bundling, integration, and ecosystem strength. The case highlights how platform logic often dominates product-level advantages.

### **Concepts Illustrated**

* Bundling as pricing strategy  
* Ecosystem competitive advantage  
* Network effects  
* Feature parity vs strategic parity

### **Key Takeaways**

* Ease of use drives early adoption (System 1 processing).  
* Bundling (“free with Office”) builds forced trial and retention.  
* Ecosystem \> standalone product.

### **Strategic Insights**

Teams vs Zoom is the classic example of:  
> *“Better product loses to better system.”*  
This is why category strategy must consider **platform dynamics**, not only consumer preference.

# **MBA 206: Marketing Study Guide**

## **MODULE 1: FUNDAMENTALS & STRATEGY**

### **1.1 Marketing Basics & Value Proposition** 

**Marketing Definition:**Marketing is a process of satisfying customer needs by creating and delivering value. It moves beyond "here's my product" (sales-driven) to "what do consumers want?" (marketing-driven).​

#### **Key Concepts:**

* **Value Proposition**: Set of benefits (physical \+ emotional) promised to satisfy customer needs. People buy expectations, not just tangible products.​  
* **Strategy vs. Tactic**s: Strategy is the vision for creating customer value; marketing plan is the roadmap with specific programs and activities.​  
* **Marketing Concept**: Consumer-centric from the outset. Answer: Who are they? What's important to them? Where in decision-making are they?​

#### **Framework – Marketing Process (5Cs):**

1. **Customer**: Descriptive traits, usage behavior, benefits sought, decision stage  
2. **Company**: Core competencies, resources, expertise, competitive advantage potential  
3. **Competition**: Current/potential competitors, new entrants, substitutes, differentiation potential (from customer POV)  
4. **Collaborators**: Channel partners, suppliers  
5. **Context**: Category trends, consumer/social trends, technology, economy, politics​

#### **Conversational Questions:**

* Where are we? Why are we there?  
* Where can we go? How do we get there?  
* How do we know we are there?​

### **1.2 Differentiation & Positioning** 

**Positioning Definition**:The place a product occupies in consumers' minds relative to competitors. Communicate using their language, keep it simple.​

#### **Differentiation Sources:**

* Cost differentiation (price, efficiency)  
* Physical product differences (features, performance)  
* Imagery/emotional differentiation (brand perception, aspirational value)  
* View category differently (addition by subtraction: IKEA, iPad, In-N-Out)  
* Viewing product category differently (e.g., iPad as a new category, not just a bigger iPhone)​

**Key Principle:** Differences must be meaningful to consumers—solve a problem, offer delight, provide competitive advantage in value.​

#### **Levels of Product:​**

* Core: Basic functionality (e.g., smartphone: connectivity, camera)  
* Expected: Standard augmentations (e.g., enhanced camera, sleek design)  
* Augmented: Additional features/benefits (e.g., facial recognition, waterproof, fitness tracking)  
* Potential: Future possibilities (e.g., AI, medical functionality)

#### **Product Augmentation Strategies:​**

* By addition: Add features  
* By multiplication: Expand portfolio (length vs. depth)  
* By reversal: Take something away (Apple, IKEA, In-N-Out balance tangible & emotional)

### **1.3 Brand Strategy & Architecture** 

**Brand Definition:**A brand stands independent of the product; it's a set of symbols, images, logo, design, name, in-store experience.​

**Brand Equity Definition:**Set of assets: awareness, loyal customers, quality perceptions, attitudes. Like home equity—costs to build, adds significant value. Most important asset \= loyal users, fuels ongoing repeat business and reduces dependency on ad budgets.​

#### **Brand Architecture Spectrum:​**

* **House of Brands**: Independent brands; maximizes spending efficiency but weaker individual brands and higher risk from overall brand damage  
* **Endorsed Brands**: One brand dominates (e.g., Cheerios General Mills, Dockers Levi's)  
* **Sub-brands**: Used together (e.g., Microsoft Office, Samsung Galaxy)  
* **Branded House**: Single parent brand; less efficient but limits risk by separating reputations

#### **Strategic Applications:​**

* Linkage vs. separation of brands  
* Coverage and efficiency  
* Minimize cannibalization, maximize volume

#### **Line Extension vs. Brand Extension:​**

* **Line extension**: New product within same category (e.g., different toothpaste types, mouthwash)  
* **Brand extension**: New products outside current category (e.g., Levi's clothing to shoes, Google to mobile phones)

#### **Category Management Illustration – Spotify:​**

* **Core**: Music delivery  
* **Expected**: Podcasts, personalized playlists  
* **Augmented**: Wrapped, "Only You," live show links  
* **Potential**: AI recommendations, social features  
* **Competition**: Apple bundling, Amazon entering; pressure on prices and profits

### **1.4 Product Life Cycle (PLC)** 

#### **Introduction Stage:**

* Heavy promotional spending (awareness, trial)  
* Building and filling distribution  
* Target early adopters  
* Higher prices; market leader or follower decision (pioneers expensive & risky)​

#### **Growth Stage:**

* Category growth continues  
* Competitors enter  
* Prices hold; promotion spending still high  
* Profits appear; unit costs decrease  
* Early augmentation; differentiation attempts​

#### **Maturity Stage:**

* Category growth slows/levels off  
* Profits decline  
* Strategy shift: no longer building share  
* Intense competition, overcapacity  
* New ways to gain share: new users, more volume from current users  
* Promotional strategy shifts: more promotion, less advertising  
* Product modifications and augmentation​

#### **Decline Stage:**

* Category decline (can't be reversed)  
* Products/brands CAN be revived in maturity (Converse, Old Spice—imagery driven)  
* Can still find volume/profits with smaller, targeted, niche strategies  
* Competitors exit​

## **MODULE 2: MARKET RESEARCH & CONSUMER INSIGHTS**

### **2.1 Market Research Framework**

#### **Decision Problem vs. Research Problem:**

* **Decision problem**: What business/marketing decision needs to be made?  
* **Research problem**: What information/insights might help?  
* **Key question**: What would I like to know that I don't know?​

#### **Three Research Purposes:​**

1. **Exploratory**: "What's happening?" (upfront: new category, acquisition, creative development; ongoing: check-ins)  
2. **Evaluative**: "Go/no-go decision" (upfront: product, advertising, packaging; concepts pre-launch)  
3. **Tracking**: "Trends, changes over time; did we meet goals?" (benchmark pre-wave, ongoing, post-wave)​

#### **Behaviors Measured:​**

* **Actual**: In-store POS, online purchases  
* **Observed**: Video, shop-a-longs, ethnography  
* **Reported**: Surveys, focus groups, interviews

#### **Qualitative vs. Quantitative:​**

* **Quantitative**: Large sample, closed-end questions (surveys, tracking studies)  
* **Qualitative**: In-depth, flexible, small sample (focus groups, interviews, ethnography)  
* Use together when possible

#### **Types of Data:​**

* Primary vs. Secondary  
* Custom vs. Syndicated (Nielsen, Mintel, Statista, Gartner)

#### **Additional Buyer Behavior Factors:​**

* System 1 vs. System 2 thinking (Kahneman—fast vs. slow)  
* Motivation (intrinsic vs. extrinsic)  
* Biases and confidence  
* Values, culture, subculture  
* AI now changing rapid data analysis and persona generation (still has mistakes; human synthesis still critical)

### **3.3 Distribution & Place**

**Role of Intermediaries**:Functional roles linking producers with buyers (storage, shipping, etc.). Includes distributors, agents, aggregators, retailers. Trade-off: cost vs. value added.​

#### **Channel Alternatives:​**

* **Conventional:** All parties operating independently in own interest  
* **Vertical Marketing System (VMS)**: Almost all partners working together (ownership, contractual)  
* **Two-Sided Platforms**: Tech-driven intermediaries bringing buyers and sellers together

**Disintermediation**: Eliminating intermediaries no longer adding value. Trends move in both directions; intermediaries may disintermediate others (e.g., travel agencies → Kayak, Expedia).​

**Omnichannel Strategy**: Not same for all customers; varies by shopping attitudes, effort, power. Can be "BM first," "online first," or "switchers." Consider customer experience, not just price/convenience.​

#### **Intensive vs. Exclusive Distribution:​**

* **Intensive**: Volume, penetration (imagery not critical)  
* **Exclusive**: Limited accessibility, value in experience (Lululemon, high-end)  
* **Selective**: In between (specialty retail, controlled distribution)

#### **Push vs. Pull:​**

* **Push**: Intermediaries/channel partners do the work  
* **Pull**: Consumers do the work; owned by brand  
* Strategy depends on power dynamics; "who owns the customer?"​

#### **Power in Distribution:​**

* Retailers hold significant power (Walmart, Costco)  
* Franchisees treated as customers (restaurants, autos)  
* Can manipulate distribution via tying agreements

#### **Vertical Integration:​**

* **Zara**: Complete control over product/fashion decisions, smaller quantities per store, fewer markdowns, shorter lead time, limited risk  
* Distribution-driven innovation

## **MODULE 5: CASE STUDIES & APPLICATIONS**

### **5.1 Making Freemium Work (Kumar)​**

**Core Model:** Free basic features \+ premium paid features. Free tier as acquisition engine; moderate conversion (2–5%) with high traffic optimal.​

#### **Six Key Questions:**

1. **What should be free?** Balance traffic and upgrade rates; tune iteratively (NYT: started 20 free articles/month → cut to 10\)  
2. **Do customers understand the premium offer?** Dropbox clear (storage limit); LinkedIn murky (many upgrades, unclear value)  
3. **Target conversion rate?** 1% too low (signals free too rich OR poor communication); 50% conversion might signal free not compelling enough; ideal: 2–5% with scale  
4. **Prepared for conversion lifecycle?** Early adopters less price-sensitive; conversion dips as user base expands to price-sensitive users; must account for rising marginal costs  
5. **Are users becoming evangelists?** Free users worth 15–25% of premium subscriber (via referrals); manage referral incentives  
6. **Committed to ongoing innovation?** Freemium is not just acquisition; must keep improving premium to drive later conversions (Dropbox: shared folders → sync → shared photos)​

#### **Business Examples:**

* **Dropbox:** 2GB free, clear pain point (storage limit), high conversion  
* **LinkedIn:** 4 premium tiers, unclear differentiation, lower conversion despite scale  
* **New York Times:** Paywall strategy, iterative free article limits  
* **Spotify:** Free tier with ads, premium ad-free and offline​

## **5.2 Thinking in Bets (Duke) – Decision Quality​**

**Core Insight:** Quality of a decision ≠ quality of its outcome. People confuse the two ("resulting").​

#### **Key Ideas:**

* **Resulting:** Evaluating decisions solely on outcomes (e.g., condemning Pete Carroll's Super Bowl pass because it was intercepted, ignoring that the play was statistically sound)  
* **Hindsight bias:** "I should have known" after a bad outcome; retrospective over-confidence  
* **Luck vs. skill:** Good decisions sometimes fail; bad decisions sometimes win. In poker and business, luck plays a real role.  
* **System 1 vs. System 2:** Fast, automatic reflexive thinking dominates decisions; slow, deliberative thinking is bandwidth-constrained. Both necessary; can't just shift more to deliberative.  
* **Poker as lab:** High-frequency, high-stakes decisions under time pressure teach separation of process from outcomes; poker players must learn to update beliefs probabilistically and resist emotional overreaction.​

#### **Practical Takeaways:**

* Review decisions based on reasoning and information, not just whether they "worked"  
* Expect noise in results; avoid strategy shifts based on single outcomes  
* Build decision-making habits that resist resulting and hindsight bias

## **5.3 Rebranding the Tepper School (Case)​**

**Situation**: Carnegie Mellon's business school, known for analytics & tech, needs a clearer brand promise to compete for MBA applicants.​

#### **Process:**

1. **Competitive positioning audit**: Discovered Tepper was tagged "future-oriented" and "innovation" but messaging was vague ("boundless possibilities")  
2. **Internal/external research:** Focus groups revealed Tepper seen as small, personable, collaborative, rigorous in analytics; BUT untapped positioning around "data-driven leaders" (not just "quant jocks")  
3. **Messaging hierarchy:​**  
   * **Differentiators:** Data-driven curriculum, flexible/rigorous programs, CMU tech ecosystem, leadership development, innovation hub location  
   * **Customer benefits:** Leadership, problem-solving, value creation, diversity, resilience  
   * **Core value:** "Lead your career, team, or company with confidence in a data-driven business world"  
4. **Slogan options:** Five options all emphasizing "intelligent future" and "data-driven" language; Oliver must choose based on differentiation, ownability, longevity

**Key Takeaway:** Clear internal hierarchy (differentiators → benefits → core value) precedes external taglines; slogans must be specific, durable, legally defensible, and aligned with research-backed positioning.

## **5.4 The Culture Map (Meyer) – Decision-Making Across Cultures​**

#### **Decision-Making Styles:**

* **U.S.:** Egalitarian ethos but top-down decisions; fast, revisable (capital "d" decision \= lowercase in practice)  
* **Germany**: Hierarchical structure but consensus-seeking; slow decisions that, once made, are fixed (capital "D")  
* **Japan:** Hierarchical \+ ultra-consensual via ringi system (proposals circulate with stamps of approval level-by-level; nemawashi informal root-binding beforehand); long discussion → rapid implementation​

**Mismatch Consequences:** Germans see American reversals as bad faith; Americans see German deliberation as slow/inflexible; both misinterpret cultural logic.​

#### **Practical Guidance:**

* **With consensual cultures**: expect longer decision cycles, treat decisions as hard to reverse  
* **With top-down cultures:** accept quick decisions from the boss, anticipate possible revisions​

## **5.6 21Seeds: Scaling a Breakout Brand (Case)**

**Situation:** Three female founders created an infused tequila (Cucumber Jalapeño) targeted at women. Diageo acquired it for expansion; now deciding on growth strategies. 

#### **Growth Strategy Options:**

1. **Product Innovation:**   
   * New flavor (e.g., spicy trend, chai, ginger beer trending)  
   * New variant (e.g., Reposado for fall/winter occasion)  
   * Risk: Become a "flavor mill"; dilute brand distinctiveness; cannibalization  
2. **New Sizes:**   
   * 1.75L for Club channel (Costco, Sam's Club) → volume, brand presence  
   * 1L for on-premise (bars, restaurants)  
   * 50mL trial packs → trial, seasonal bump; but margin pressure  
3. **Ready-to-Drink (RTD):**   
   * Hard seltzer market growing 17% CAGR (2023–2027)  
   * Lower margins, younger demographic, health/wellness positioning  
   * NOT directly competitive with 750mL (occasion-based)  
   * Wide retail distribution (lower alcohol \= all Target stores)  
   * Crowded category (Truly, High Noon, White Claw, non-traditional entrants)  
4. **On-Premise Expansion:**   
   * Currently 14% on-premise mix; industry norm \~20%  
   * 21Seeds Spicy Margarita \= simple, consistent, cost-effective for bars  
   * Goal: Get on menu (not just back bar) as "21Seeds Spicy Margarita"  
   * Challenges: Fragmented market, regional decision-making, hand-to-hand combat with bar staff  
5. **Brand Positioning Shift:**   
   * Current: Focused on women (original brand story: three moms)  
   * Research shows: Men also like the taste; brand over-indexes with sports viewers  
   * Tension: Expand to men without alienating female core? Or stick with "moms" \= OG influencers?  
   * Risk: Over-broadening; loss of distinctiveness  
6. **Celebrity Endorsement:**   
   * Industry trend: 40 celebrity brands (2018) → 350+ (2023)  
   * Success examples: Casamigos (Clooney), Teremana (The Rock—1M cases/year in 3 years), 818 Tequila (Kendall Jenner)  
   * Reach: Rock has 392M Instagram followers  
   * ROI: 24% of adults more likely to buy; 4% immediate sales bump  
   * Cost: Expensive; requires authentic commitment (e.g., JLo's RTD spritz—but JLo doesn't drink, husband recovering alcoholic)  
   * Risk: Overshadows founding story  
7. **Media Buying Strategy:**   
   * Targeted media buys (BravoCon campaign): curated timing, podcasts, social media; high CPM, limited scale but surround-sound to target audience  
   * Programmatic buying: Algorithmic efficiency, lower CPM, scale; less control, based on Diageo's broader portfolio benchmarks  
   * KPI debate: Impressions (reach, scale) vs. engagement (click-through, conversion, intent to buy)  
   * Hantas argues engagement drives velocity at retail (90% of revenue tied to distribution)  
8. **International Expansion:**   
   * Currently 47 U.S. states, no international  
   * 85% of tequila consumed in U.S./Mexico  
   * First-mover advantage in infused tequila internationally  
   * Risk: Spreading thin; still huge U.S. growth potential

#### **Competitive Landscape:** 

* Cuervo 1800: Coconut \+ new Cucumber Jalapeño flavor  
* Total Wine private labels: Antao, El Padrino flavored tequilas  
* Painted Donkey: 3 flavors  
* Pressure mounting; need to move quickly

**Key Tension:** Founder vision (focused on brand story, women, engagement) vs. Diageo's growth mandate (scale, reach, portfolio leverage).
