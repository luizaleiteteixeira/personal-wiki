# Examples in the Wild 2

I used to work for Egon Zehnder, a leadership advisory firm focused on executive search. I remember how hard it was to price a project, especially because it was difficult to measure the marginal cost of a search. 

"Should we accept this project?" was a frequent discussion. 

As we learned with John Deree’s case, we shouldn’t include overhead costs (like Linkedin Recruiting license, rent, and salary of support staff) in the marginal cost. The right measure should then be the incremental hours that consultants and experts would spend on the work and other direct expenses, such as travel. However, estimating that cost was challenging, as we never knew how long it would take to find the right person for the position. There were also "hidden factors" like past training, accumulated experience, or prior similar projects that sometimes made consultants faster. And the opportunity cost of using a senior leader's time was hard to quantify, since we couldn’t predict future demand (project’s type, duration, price). 

As a result, pricing decisions couldn’t rely purely on costs. It required judgment, benchmarks, and the understanding that the “true” marginal cost was hard to determine prior to the start of a project.
